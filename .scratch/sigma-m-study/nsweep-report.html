Picking n between 2N and 4N

The legacy plan rounds the bandwidth N up to a power of two and doubles it, so the oversampling σ = n/N lands anywhere in [2, 4). Sweeping every integer n across that whole band, for six representative N, shows the cost is governed almost entirely by the largest prime factor of n — not by σ. A rough n costs about three times as much as a smooth one.

Time across the band

One point per integer n from 2N to 4N, shaded by the largest prime factor of n. The pale points at the floor are the sizes the FFT likes; the dense band above them is everything else. The floor is flat across the whole range — more oversampling buys no speed, it only costs memory.

Why: the largest prime factor of n

Every measured size, pooled across the six N and normalised to that row's own n = 2N time. Sizes whose largest prime factor is 13 or below stay within a quarter of the baseline. Past that the median more than triples — and 6389 of the 7054 sizes in the band are in that group, which is why landing on a good n matters more than landing on a small one.

What the extra oversampling does buy

Accuracy at a fixed cut-off m = 6, every measured size from all six N plotted together. They collapse onto a single curve: the error depends on σ alone, not on N or on how smooth n is. Going from σ = 2 to σ = 4 buys roughly two orders of magnitude — real, but normally you would rather spend it by lowering m than by quadrupling the grid.

Four ways to choose n

Per bandwidth: the naive 2N, what the legacy plan picks, the smallest 5-smooth n ≥ 2N, and the fastest size anywhere in the band. Times are the mean forward transform over 100 repetitions.

What this says about the legacy rule

The legacy rule is sound most of the time, and its instinct is right: it is buying a smooth n, and paying oversampling to get it. For N = 509, a prime, it turns a 3× penalty into nothing for 1.2 % extra oversampling. Its weakness is the case where N sits just above a power of two. At N = 517 it jumps to n = 2048, σ = 3.96 — nearly twice the grid of the 5-smooth pick at n = 1080, σ = 2.09, and 1.34× slower into the bargain, because a bigger power of two still costs more than a smaller smooth size. Choosing the smallest 5-smooth n ≥ 2N matched or beat the legacy rule at every N measured here, and never needed σ above 2.09.

Method

Double precision, Kaiser-Bessel window, 1-D, m = 6 fixed so only n varies, M = 2N nodes drawn once per N. Each size is planned through plan_ng with NFFT_ESTIMATE | NFFT_NO_DIRECT and FFTW_ESTIMATE; timings are the mean of 100 executions after a warm-up. Error is max_j |f_j - s_j| / ||f_hat||_1 against a long-double direct NDFT, computed once per N since it does not depend on n.

FFTW planning is ESTIMATE, which is what a one-shot caller gets. FFTW_MEASURE would narrow the gap between rough and smooth sizes at the cost of planning time; the ordering of the four rules would not change. Panel y-axes are clipped at each row's 99th percentile so the floor stays readable; clipped points sit on the top edge.

Driver, data and analysis: .scratch/sigma-m-study/nsweep.c, nanalyze.py, nsmooth.py.
